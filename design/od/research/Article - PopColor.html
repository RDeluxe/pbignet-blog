One red thing

A valley of greys and greens, and a bridge someone decided to paint the loudest red they could buy. The eye has no choice.

Filed by Pierrick Bignet · Engineer by trade, artist by default

Most of the frame is weather. Grey cloud, grey water, a wall of wet green hills. Then a steel suspension bridge in a red so saturated it looks pasted in. It is not subtle and it does not want to be.

The photograph had already made its decision. All I had to do was agree with it. The treatment follows: everything that is mood gets dithered down into the ground, a textured field of dark olive. The one thing that carries the picture keeps its colour. Nothing else competes.

01Find the one thing

Every frame worth keeping has a hierarchy, and most treatments flatten it. A filter grades the whole image at once; it cannot tell the bridge from the weather. The spot treatment can, because it keys on the one property the subject does not share with its context: hue.

Key the subject by hue, not by hand. No lasso, no path drawn around the girders. Each pixel's chroma is projected onto the kept colour's direction, and the ones that score above the threshold are composited back in full colour, with a soft edge wide enough to read as a halo rather than a cut-out.

The whole frame is dithered into a duotone ground first — dark olive ink on a lifted olive paper. The masked subject returns on top in full colour. No flat colour block, no second accent, no hand-drawn mask to maintain when the crop changes. One red thing.

02Why it reads

It works because restraint is legible. A picture that is loud everywhere is loud nowhere. Take the colour out of the parts that were only ever context, and the part that mattered does the rest of the work for free.

A picture that is loud everywhere is loud nowhere.

There is a printing ancestor to this. Risograph and spot-ink jobs never had the budget for full colour; they had a black plate and one drum of something loud. The constraint produced the style: texture carries the tone, one ink carries the meaning. The dither is that black plate. The bridge is the drum.

03The recipe travels

Because the key is a hue and two numbers, the treatment is portable. A maple in autumn, a single lit window at dusk, a red umbrella on a grey street — each needs its own threshold and its own ground, but none needs new machinery. Find the one thing. Dither the weather. Let the colour mean something.

The numbers for this frame are in the plate above, named as the component takes them. When the next photograph disagrees with these values — and it will — the recipe changes, not the method.
